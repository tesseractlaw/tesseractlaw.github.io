Who Owns the Moon?
India's Position Under
the Outer Space Treaty

Four nations are actively returning to the lunar surface. Private companies are planning mines. The Outer Space Treaty says no one can "appropriate" the Moon. That word is doing a lot of work — and nobody has tested it in court yet.

Shubhanshu Gupta November 2026 · 8 min read

In September 2023, Chandrayaan-3's Vikram lander touched down near the lunar south pole. India became the fourth nation to soft-land on the Moon — and the first to do so near a region scientists believe is rich in water ice. That ice, if extractable, is not just scientifically interesting. It is rocket fuel. It is life support. It is the reason everyone is suddenly heading south.

The legal question that follows immediately: who, if anyone, owns what is under that regolith?

The Treaty That Governs Outer Space

The 1967 Outer Space Treaty (OST) is the foundational document of international space law. India is a signatory. So are the United States, Russia, China, the United Kingdom, and over 110 other nations. Its core provision on ownership is Article II:

"Outer space, including the Moon and other celestial bodies, is not subject to national appropriation by claim of sovereignty, by means of use or occupation, or by any other means."

Clear enough, it seems. No one owns the Moon. No flag planted on the surface creates title. No nation can annex a crater. The Moon is the "province of all mankind" — a phrase from Article I that is as aspirational as it sounds, and about as legally enforceable as it looks.

But here is the question that Article II does not answer, and which has become the central dispute of modern space law: does the prohibition on national appropriation also prohibit the extraction and private ownership of resources?

The American Position: Mining Is Fine

In 2015, the United States passed the Commercial Space Launch Competitiveness Act, which explicitly granted US citizens the right to own resources extracted from celestial bodies. The legal logic: you cannot own the Moon, but you can own what you dig out of it. Think of it like the high seas — no nation owns the ocean, but a fishing company can own the fish it catches.

In 2020, the Trump administration formalised this further through Executive Order 13914, stating that the US does not view outer space as a "global commons" and that international law does not prohibit resource extraction.

Treaty Ref. The Artemis Accords — bilateral agreements signed by the US and partner nations — operationalise this position, establishing "safety zones" around lunar operations and affirming the right to extract resources. As of November 2026, 43 nations have signed. India has not.

The Counter-Position: The Moon Agreement

In 1979, the Moon Agreement attempted to close the loophole. It declared lunar resources to be the "common heritage of mankind" and called for an international regime to govern their exploitation — meaning any resource extraction would require international oversight and benefit-sharing.

It went almost nowhere. The major spacefaring nations — the US, Russia, China — never ratified it. India signed but never ratified either. The Moon Agreement currently has 18 state parties, none of whom are actively conducting lunar operations. It is, in practical terms, a dead letter.

Where India Actually Stands

India's position is notably — and strategically — ambiguous.

India has not signed the Artemis Accords. India did not ratify the Moon Agreement. India is a party to the Outer Space Treaty, the Rescue Agreement, the Liability Convention, and the Registration Convention — the core UN space law framework. Beyond that, India has not committed to either the American "extraction is fine" position or the developing-world "common heritage" position that many Global South nations favour.

This ambiguity is not an accident. India is simultaneously a space power with real lunar assets — the south pole landing site has been officially named Shiv Shakti Point — and a nation that has historically championed developing-country interests in international forums. Picking a side has costs in both directions.

India landed near the lunar south pole. That is not a symbolic achievement. It is a territorial claim in everything but legal name.

The Parliamentary Question Hanging Over All of This

In March 2026, India's Parliamentary Standing Committee recommended the enactment of a national space legislation. As of now, India has no Space Act. The commercial space sector — 399 startups and counting — operates under a policy-led framework through IN-SPACe (Indian National Space Promotion and Authorisation Centre), which was created by executive order, not statute.

A national space law, when it comes, will have to answer the resource question. It will have to decide whether Indian companies can own extracted lunar resources, what authorisation they require, and what liability the government bears if something goes wrong. These are not distant hypotheticals. At least two Indian startups are already working on deep-space missions.

India's Legal Gap Unlike the US (2015 Commercial Space Launch Competitiveness Act), Luxembourg (2017 Space Resources Law), UAE (2020 Space Law), and Japan (2021 Space Resources Act), India has no statutory framework for commercial space resource rights. The proposed Space Activities Bill — versions of which have been in draft since 2017 — has not been enacted.

The Honest Answer

Who owns the Moon? Nobody, under the Outer Space Treaty. Can you own what you mine from it? Under US, Luxembourgish, and UAE law, yes. Under Indian law — there is no answer yet, because there is no law.

What we do know is that the question is no longer academic. The south pole of the Moon contains water ice. Water ice is rocket fuel. The nation — or company — that controls access to that resource will have an extraordinary strategic advantage in everything that follows. India put a lander there. India has not yet put a law there.

That is the gap worth watching.
