Who Owns the Moon? India's Position Under the Outer Space Treaty

Shubhanshu Gupta November 2026 · 8 min read

Several countries are heading back to the Moon and private companies are planning to mine it. The Outer Space Treaty says nobody can appropriate the Moon. That one word carries most of the legal weight, and no court has ever had to interpret it.

In August 2023, Chandrayaan-3's Vikram lander touched down near the lunar south pole. India became the fourth country to soft-land on the Moon, and the first to land that close to the south pole, where scientists think there is water ice in permanently shadowed craters. If that ice can be extracted, it can be turned into drinking water, oxygen and rocket fuel. That is why so many missions are now aimed at the same region.

So the legal question follows quickly. Who, if anyone, owns what lies under the lunar soil?

The treaty that governs outer space

The 1967 Outer Space Treaty is the base of international space law. India is a party, as are the United States, Russia, China and more than a hundred other countries. Article II deals with ownership:

"Outer space, including the Moon and other celestial bodies, is not subject to national appropriation by claim of sovereignty, by means of use or occupation, or by any other means."

On a first reading that settles it. No country owns the Moon, planting a flag gives no title, and no state can annex a crater. Article I adds that the exploration and use of outer space is "the province of all mankind", which sounds grand but is hard to enforce.

What Article II does not say is whether the ban on national appropriation also stops companies from extracting resources and owning them. That gap is now the main argument in space law.

The American position: extraction is allowed

In 2015 the United States passed the Commercial Space Launch Competitiveness Act. It gives US citizens the right to own resources they extract from asteroids and other celestial bodies. The reasoning is that you cannot own the Moon, but you can own what you take out of it, much like a fishing company owns its catch even though nobody owns the high seas.

In April 2020, Executive Order 13914 went further. It said the US does not treat outer space as a global commons and that international law does not prohibit resource extraction.

The Artemis Accords The Artemis Accords are a set of non-binding principles led by the US. Signatories accept that resource extraction is consistent with the Outer Space Treaty and agree to "safety zones" around lunar activities. India signed the Accords in June 2023.

The other view: the Moon Agreement

The 1979 Moon Agreement tried to close the gap. It called lunar resources the "common heritage of mankind" and asked for an international regime to govern their use, which would mean international oversight and some sharing of benefits.

It never took off. The US, Russia and China never joined. India signed it in 1982 but never ratified it. Only a small number of countries are parties, and almost none of them has an active lunar programme. Saudi Arabia, one of the few that had joined, withdrew in 2023.

Where India stands

India's position is somewhere in between, and that looks deliberate.

India is a party to the four core UN treaties: the Outer Space Treaty, the Rescue Agreement, the Liability Convention and the Registration Convention. It has signed the Artemis Accords. It has not ratified the Moon Agreement. But it has not passed any law of its own that says whether an Indian company can own resources taken from the Moon.

India has real interests on both sides. It now has its own landing site near the south pole, officially named Shiv Shakti Point. It has also spent decades speaking for developing countries in international forums, where the "common heritage" idea has strong support. Committing fully to either side would cost something.

India put a lander near the lunar south pole. It has not yet put a law there.

The missing piece: a national space law

India still has no Space Act. Private space companies work under policy documents, mainly the Indian Space Policy 2023, and get their authorisations from IN-SPACe, a body set up by government decision rather than by statute. A Space Activities Bill has been in draft since 2017 and has not been passed.

Whenever that law comes, it will have to deal with resources. It will need to say whether Indian companies can own what they extract, what permission they need first, and how much liability the government takes on if something goes wrong.

Countries that have legislated The United States (2015), Luxembourg (2017), the UAE (2019) and Japan (2021) have passed laws that deal with rights over space resources. India has no equivalent yet.

So, who owns the Moon?

Under the Outer Space Treaty, nobody. Can you own what you mine from it? Under US, Luxembourg, UAE and Japanese law, yes. Under Indian law there is no answer yet, because there is no law.

The question is no longer theoretical. The south pole holds water ice, water ice can become rocket fuel, and whoever controls access to it will have a real advantage in everything that follows. That makes it worth watching what India decides.
