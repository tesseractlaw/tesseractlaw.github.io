Your Satellite Launch Contract Has a Force Majeure Clause. It Won't Work the Way You Think.

Shubhanshu Gupta November 2026 7 min read
← Back to all posts

Every satellite launch contract I have reviewed for Indian space startups has a force majeure clause. Every single one. And almost every founder who shows me that clause believes it protects them from launch delays caused by regulatory holds, ISRO schedule conflicts, or range unavailability.

It almost certainly does not. Here is why that matters, and what you should actually negotiate for.

The Core Problem: Broad Language, Narrow Courts

Force majeure clauses in Indian contracts tend to be drafted wide. Very wide. You will find language that covers "acts of God, government actions, regulatory restrictions, natural disasters, epidemics, wars, civil unrest, failure of third-party suppliers" — sometimes stretching across half a page.

Founders read that list and feel covered. The problem is that Indian courts read the same list and ask a completely different question: was performance actually impossible, or was it merely delayed, more expensive, or commercially inconvenient?

The governing principle, from Energy Watchdog v. CERC (2017): Force majeure under Indian law does not rescue a party from a bad commercial outcome. It applies only where performance has become legally or physically impossible — not where it has become harder, costlier, or delayed.

This is not a technicality. It is the reason most force majeure claims in India fail — in power purchase agreements, in construction contracts, and in a hypothetical satellite launch dispute that an Indian court will one day decide.

What COVID Taught Us About Force Majeure Clauses

The pandemic was the stress test that exposed every drafting flaw in every Indian commercial contract. Courts were flooded with force majeure defences. And something interesting happened: parties that had been invoking force majeure to avoid paying rent, to escape delivery timelines, to get out of contracts that had simply become unprofitable — found that the defence was not available to them.

Indian courts consistently asked: were you actually prevented from performing? Or were you using force majeure to avoid a contract that COVID made inconvenient?

What the COVID cases also revealed was something lawyers had known but rarely discussed with clients: many parties invoking force majeure were already in breach of material obligations before the pandemic hit. They had missed payment timelines, failed to meet technical milestones, or quietly stopped performing. When COVID arrived, it became a convenient explanation for a default that had its own independent origins.

Courts saw through this. Force majeure does not cure an existing breach. If your obligation to pay the launch service provider was already 90 days overdue when the launch window was delayed, you cannot retroactively blame the delay for your non-payment.

The rule that catches founders off-guard: Force majeure is not a time machine. It excuses future non-performance caused by the qualifying event. It does not reach back and cure obligations that were already breached before the event occurred.

Now Apply This to Satellite Launches in India

India's space sector is operating in a genuinely novel regulatory environment. IN-SPACe — the Indian National Space Promotion and Authorisation Centre — issues clearances for launch campaigns, satellite operations, and ground-station deployment. The framework under the Indian Space Policy 2023 is still being settled. The Space Activities Bill, long overdue, underwent further drafting as recently as mid-2025.

In this environment, delays are not exceptional. They are practically built into the structure of doing business in Indian space right now. And that is precisely the problem with relying on force majeure to address them.

SCENARIO: IN-SPACe places a regulatory hold on your launch

Your launch contract is signed. The launch window is set. IN-SPACe then holds your clearance — perhaps because your satellite carries a payload with dual-use technology concerns, perhaps because spectrum coordination with another operator is unresolved, perhaps simply because the regulatory queue is long.

Your launch service provider says you owe them a standing charge for every month the launch is delayed. You say force majeure. What does an Indian court do with this?

Almost certainly, it does not give you the relief you expect. Here is why.

First, a regulatory hold by IN-SPACe is not unforeseeable. India's space regulatory framework is new, underdeveloped, and openly acknowledged to be a work in progress. Everyone entering a launch contract in India today is on notice that regulatory clearances may take longer than anticipated, may require additional documentation, may be revised mid-process. Courts will ask: given that this risk was entirely foreseeable, why did your contract not address it specifically?

Second, a regulatory hold does not make performance permanently impossible. It makes performance delayed. Indian courts, following the standard set in Alopi Parshad & Sons v. Union of India (1960), draw a sharp distinction between impossibility and difficulty. A launch delayed by 8 months due to a regulatory hold is commercially painful. It is not legally impossible. The satellite can still be launched. The contract can still be performed.

Third — and this is my practitioner instinct, not settled law — Indian courts would likely treat a nascent industry differently from one where launch failures qualify as extraordinary events. Rocket launches are not part of daily commercial life in India the way shipping containers or airline freight are. The judiciary does not yet have an intuition for what is "normal" risk in this sector. That cuts both ways: courts may be sympathetic, but they may also be uninformed. That is a bad combination for litigation strategy.

The underlying logic: SpaceX can claim a launch delay is force majeure partly because commercial launches are now routine enough to have a baseline. When launches become part of ordinary Indian commercial life — when Agnikul and Skyroot are flying dozens of missions a year — courts will develop a sense of what constitutes an abnormal disruption. Until then, everything looks foreseeable.

What Your Contract Should Actually Say

Force majeure is a last resort. The better approach is to negotiate specific, defined mechanisms into the contract before you need them. Here is what I look for when reviewing launch contracts for space founders:

1. Regulatory delay as a defined event — not force majeure

Draft a separate clause that deals specifically with regulatory authorisation delays. It should specify: (a) what counts as a regulatory delay event, with IN-SPACe, DoT, and spectrum clearances named explicitly; (b) a notice period within which the invoking party must communicate the delay; (c) a rescheduling mechanism with defined timelines; and (d) the allocation of standing charges during the delay period.

If this sits inside your force majeure clause, it will be interpreted narrowly. If it sits in its own clause as a defined commercial risk allocation, it has a better chance of being applied as written.

2. Mutual obligation to mitigate

Indian courts are unforgiving toward parties who invoke force majeure but do nothing to reduce the impact. Your clause should explicitly require both parties to take reasonable steps to mitigate the effect of a qualifying event. If you can demonstrate you actively engaged with IN-SPACe to expedite clearance, that matters. If you simply waited and billed the other side, courts and arbitrators will notice.

3. Notice requirements — and take them seriously

Typical clause language (too vague)
"The affected party shall notify the other party promptly upon the occurrence of a force majeure event."

"Promptly" is not a timeline. In practice, founders wait — hoping the problem resolves itself — and give notice only when it is clear the delay is serious. By that point, the contractual notice window has often passed. Indian courts treat failure to give timely notice as a waiver of the force majeure defence. The clause should specify a number of days: 7 days from when the party knew or ought to have known.

4. Define what "regulatory action" means — and what it doesn't

A force majeure clause that covers "government action" without further definition creates problems in both directions. It could be read to cover a routine IN-SPACe processing delay (which should not qualify). It could fail to cover a sudden policy reversal or launch moratorium (which should qualify). Be specific. The clause should distinguish between: (a) a regulatory action that makes launch legally prohibited, and (b) a regulatory process that is simply slow.

The Harder Question: What Are You Actually Trying to Protect?

When I ask founders what they want force majeure to do in their launch contract, the answer is almost always: "If a launch gets delayed for reasons outside my control, I don't want to pay the launch provider for that delay."

That is a perfectly legitimate commercial position. The problem is that force majeure is a poor mechanism for achieving it. Force majeure is a binary tool — it either applies or it doesn't, and if it applies, it suspends the contract. What founders actually need is a risk-sharing mechanism: a clause that allocates delay costs differently depending on who caused the delay and why.

That is not a force majeure clause. That is a schedule management and delay allocation clause, which should sit elsewhere in the contract entirely.

India's space sector is growing fast. IN-SPACe is developing its processes. ISRO's commercial arm is evolving. The legal framework is being written in real time. In that environment, contracts that rely on generic boilerplate — however elegantly drafted — will not hold up when things go wrong.

Write for the specific risks. Name the specific regulators. Allocate the specific costs. That is what contracts are for.
